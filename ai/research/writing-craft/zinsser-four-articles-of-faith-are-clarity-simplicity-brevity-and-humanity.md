## CLAIMS

- William Zinsser wrote that his four principles of good English are clarity, simplicity, brevity, and humanity. [zinsser-american-scholar]
- A contemporaneous/primary-book attribution is preferable to treating the four-item list as a modern writing-coach mnemonic: *On Writing Well* is Zinsser's book on nonfiction writing and is published under his name. [zinsser-primary-book]
- The order varies across reproductions, but Zinsser's own essay gives clarity, simplicity, brevity, and humanity in that order. [zinsser-american-scholar] [zinsser-secondary-quote]
- “Five principles of good writing” summaries that append logic are later instructional summaries, not evidence that Zinsser's named four were five. [later-five-principles]
- Zinsser presents the four as quality aims and explains them through clear meaning, simple language, economy, and the writer's recognizable human presence. [zinsser-american-scholar]

## SYNTHESIS

The answer to the skill-design question is yes: clarity, simplicity, brevity, and humanity are the canonical four to attribute to Zinsser. Primary-source provenance is strong because Zinsser states all four directly in his 2009 essay for *The American Scholar*; the publisher page independently confirms his authorship of *On Writing Well*.

For an agent-writing skill, use the four as a review compass: make the reader's meaning easy to recover (clarity), remove needless complexity and jargon (simplicity), cut material that does no work (brevity), and write to a real person in a recognizable, non-mechanical voice (humanity). Treat them as coupled checks, not independent quotas: brevity must not delete necessary conditions, simplicity must not erase exact technical terms, and “humanity” must not become casualness or invented personal experience. “Logic” may be a useful additional review dimension, but label it as an adaptation or later summary rather than “Zinsser's fifth principle.”
