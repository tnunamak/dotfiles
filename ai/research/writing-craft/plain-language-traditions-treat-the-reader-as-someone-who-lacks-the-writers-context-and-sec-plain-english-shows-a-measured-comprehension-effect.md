## CLAIMS

- Zinsser names "clutter" as the disease of American writing and defines it as "the language people speak when they're too scared to say what they mean," giving euphemism examples (slum → "depressed socioeconomic area," garbage collector → "waste disposal personnel") that are specifically insider/institutional language opaque to an outside reader. [zinsser-clutter-litcharts]
- Zinsser's remedy is mechanical, not a vague "write clearly" instruction: reexamine each sentence, ask whether every word is doing new work, and prune ruthlessly what isn't. [zinsser-clutter-litcharts]
- Orwell's essay "Politics and the English Language" (1946) ends with six explicit rules: (1) never use a metaphor/simile/figure of speech you're used to seeing in print; (2) never use a long word where a short one will do; (3) if it is possible to cut a word out, always cut it out; (4) never use the passive where you can use the active; (5) never use a foreign phrase, scientific word, or jargon word if an everyday English equivalent exists; (6) break any of these rules sooner than say anything outright barbarous. [orwell-foundation-six-rules]
- Orwell frames the underlying problem as language used to conceal rather than express thought, arguing political language specifically is "designed to make lies sound truthful and murder respectable." [orwell-foundation-six-rules]
- The Plain Writing Act of 2010 defines plain writing as writing that is clear, concise, well-organized, and follows best practices appropriate to the subject/field AND the intended audience — audience-fit is written into the statutory definition, not just style guidance. [plain-writing-act-definition]
- plainlanguage.gov explicitly rejects "dumbing down" as the goal; its stated first rule is to write for the audience's actual existing knowledge level, whether that audience is an 8th-grade class or PhD candidates, and it explicitly warns writers not to guess or assume who the audience is. [plainlanguage-gov-audience-myth]
- plainlanguage.gov distinguishes necessary technical terms from jargon: a technical term is necessary when there is no other correct way to express the idea (its example: "brinulator valve control ring"), while jargon is technical-sounding language used where an everyday equivalent exists; the guidance is to minimize jargon but define genuinely necessary technical terms, keeping definitions brief and preferring in-text definition over a front- or back-loaded glossary section. [plainlanguage-gov-jargon-github]
- The SEC adopted Rule 421(d), the "Plain English" disclosure rule, effective October 1, 1998, requiring the cover page, summary, and risk-factors section of prospectuses to be written in plain English, accompanied by *A Plain English Handbook* with a preface by Warren Buffett describing his own inability to decipher public companies' filings despite decades of reading them. [sec-plain-english-handbook]
- A study of Form 424/IPO prospectus and 10-K filings from 1994–2009 found the SEC's plain English rule produced a measurable, substantive change in issuers' disclosure style, with firms having stronger corporate governance more likely to comply. [sec-plain-english-10k-study]
- A textual analysis of over 56,000 10-K filings from 1994–2006 found firms whose filings improved more under plain-English compliance saw a corresponding increase in small-investor trading participation, holding the market-wide trading trend constant. [sec-plain-english-10k-study]
- A separate study estimated that moving a bond issuer's disclosure readability from the 75th to the 50th percentile (i.e., making it more readable) would save roughly $440,000 annually in interest on a $430 million face-value bond, tying disclosure readability directly to a market-priced cost-of-capital effect. [sec-plain-english-bond-cost-study]
- A 2000 U.S. government case study reported that plain-language editing halved the length/complexity of the Farm Credit Administration's FOIA request procedures and that the Department of Transportation reorganized its drug/alcohol testing rules (covering several hundred thousand employers and 8 million employees) into a question-and-answer format with charts. [npr-2000-case-study]

## SYNTHESIS

All three traditions converge on the same underlying move: treat the reader as someone who was never in the room where the writer's knowledge was built, and design against that gap rather than assume it away. Zinsser names the failure mode ("clutter" as fear-driven or institutional insider language — euphemism is jargon's socially acceptable cousin), Orwell gives a mechanical checklist for stripping it (rules 1, 2, and 5 are all specific instances of "don't use language only insiders recognize"), and the federal plain-language tradition turns it into a testable audience-identification step: know what the reader already knows, and write the bridge from there to what they need, rather than writing from what *you* know outward. None of them offer a psychological theory of the "curse of knowledge" by that name — that's a later cognitive-bias framing (Heath brothers / Pinker) that the plain-language movement's practices independently anticipated by decades.

The jargon-vs-precision tension resolves the same way across sources: the target is not "no technical terms," it's "no *unnecessary* technical terms," with necessity defined narrowly (no correct everyday substitute exists) and precision preserved by defining the term once, briefly, in place — not by a glossary the reader has to round-trip to. This directly answers the worry that plain-language editing degrades technical accuracy: the rule isn't "replace domain vocabulary," it's "earn every domain word by first checking whether a plain synonym loses information."

The empirical record is thinner than plainlanguage.gov's own marketing implies — there is no single before/after RCT showing "readability score X points lower produced Y% comprehension gain" on a specific government form in these results. The strongest quantified evidence found is financial-market-based (the SEC 10-K studies), which is indirect: it shows behavior changes (more small-investor participation, lower cost of capital) correlated with measured readability improvement post-1998, not a controlled comprehension test. That's still a real, citable, market-priced effect — useful as the best available "plain language changes real-world outcomes, not just reader sentiment" evidence — but it should be cited as correlational disclosure-behavior evidence, not as a controlled comprehension experiment. If a future search needs a true controlled comprehension study, the informed-consent-form literature (simplified vs. standard consent forms) is a more directly experimental adjacent body of evidence worth a follow-up entry.
