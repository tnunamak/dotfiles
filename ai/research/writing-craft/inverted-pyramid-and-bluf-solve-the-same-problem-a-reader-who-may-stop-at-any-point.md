## CLAIMS

- The inverted pyramid places the most newsworthy fact first and supporting detail after in descending order of importance, structurally the reverse of chronological narrative. [poynter-birth-of-inverted-pyramid]
- The form is generally traced to American wire-service reporting in the second half of the 19th century, when telegraph transmission was expensive (as much as a penny per character) and unreliable. [poynter-birth-of-inverted-pyramid]
- The popular story that the inverted pyramid arose because Civil War correspondents needed the story to survive a cut telegraph wire is treated by journalism historians as a contested "myth," not settled history — researchers found many Civil War-era dispatches still written chronologically, in the older narrative style, not "first news first." [poynter-birth-of-inverted-pyramid]
- Journalism historian David T. Z. Mindich argues the inverted pyramid was effectively born with coverage of Lincoln's assassination on April 15, 1865 — Secretary of War Edwin Stanton's telegram, printed in the New York Herald, opened with the essential fact (the shooting) rather than a chronological buildup. [poynter-birth-of-inverted-pyramid]
- Economic pressure, not just wire reliability, drove the shift: newspapers spent enormous sums on Civil War-era telegraph costs, which pushed writing toward being concise and stripped of ornament and opinion. [poynter-birth-of-inverted-pyramid]
- The Associated Press, one of the first wire services, adopted an internal agreement that stories be brief, written for a national audience, and deliberately stripped of the regional partisanship common in 19th-century American papers — a form later scholars (e.g., James Carey) credit with helping institutionalize "objective" journalism. [poynter-birth-of-inverted-pyramid]
- A documented practical payoff of the inverted pyramid, independent of its origin story: it lets editors cut a story from the bottom under space constraints without rewriting it, because the least important material is always at the end. [poynter-birth-of-inverted-pyramid]
- The journalistic "lede" (deliberately misspelled to avoid confusion with hot-metal "lead" typesetting slugs in production) is the opening of a news story, and its job is to state the story's most important fact and hook the reader into continuing. [wikipedia-lead-paragraph]
- Convention teaches that a strong lede answers brief versions of who, what, when, where, why, and often how (the "Five Ws and H") about the story's key event. [wikipedia-lead-paragraph]
- AP's own internal writing guidance has historically pushed back against treating the Five Ws as a rigid checklist: a mid-20th-century AP writing booklet instructed writers to "work out the most interesting arrangement... forget the traditional five Ws if an offbeat technique will hook the reader," prioritizing engagement and clarity over mechanically covering all five. [cjr-ap-stylebook-history]
- The AP's 1951 Writing Handbook framed the standard as readability: writing that "informs the reader clearly and quickly, completely and interestingly," with every lead and paragraph judged against that bar rather than a fixed template. [cjr-ap-stylebook-history]
- "Burying the lede" (or "burying the lead") is the named failure mode: not putting a story's most important, interesting, or attention-grabbing fact in the opening, instead surfacing it later where readers may not reach it — done sometimes deliberately (to stretch engagement or soften an inconvenient fact), sometimes by accident. [wikipedia-lead-paragraph]
- BLUF ("Bottom Line Up Front") is a U.S. Army writing standard codified in Army Regulation 25-50 ("Information Management: Records Management: Preparing and Managing Correspondence"), which states Army writing must be concise, organized, and put the main point — the bottom line — at the very beginning of the correspondence. [ar-25-50-legalclarity]
- AR 25-50 pairs BLUF with a second non-negotiable requirement: active voice, on the grounds that it removes ambiguity about who is responsible for what — a concern for accountability specifically, not just readability. [ar-25-50-legalclarity]
- Secondary sourcing explicitly describes BLUF as paralleling journalism's inverted pyramid: both are "deductive" structures that put the conclusion before the supporting reasoning, as opposed to an "inductive" structure that builds up to a conclusion at the end. [wikipedia-bluf]
- BLUF has spread beyond the military into executive summaries, tagged-subject-line email (INFO/ACTION/DECISION), policy memos for time-constrained readers, technical writing, and even clinical handoff communication in healthcare, where it is credited with reducing errors caused by information getting lost in a long report. [wikipedia-bluf]
- U.S. federal agencies are separately bound by the Plain Writing Act of 2010, which requires "clear, concise, well-organized" writing "appropriate to the subject or field and intended audience" — a government-wide plain-language mandate that AR 25-50's BLUF requirement predates and exemplifies rather than derives from. [ar-25-50-legalclarity]

## SYNTHESIS

Two independent traditions — 19th-century wire journalism and 20th-century military correspondence — converged on the identical structural fix for the identical problem: a reader (or a downstream cutting editor, or a commander reading the 30th memo before lunch) may stop consuming the document at any point, and may not share the writer's accumulated context for why the details matter. Both traditions resolve this the same way: state the single most important fact as literally the first sentence, then descend in order of decreasing importance, so that truncation from the bottom never removes the point.

The popular origin story for the inverted pyramid (Civil War reporters racing a cut telegraph wire) is worth citing carefully — Poynter itself flags it as contested folklore rather than settled fact, with counter-evidence of chronological Civil War dispatches. The more defensible causal claim is economic and organizational: per-character telegraph costs, plus the AP's institutional need for brief, non-partisan copy usable by papers of every stripe, pushed writing toward front-loaded, opinion-stripped prose. The Lincoln-assassination telegram (1865) is the most concretely cited early exemplar, not proof of a single deliberate invention. Treat "telegraph line got cut" as a memorable-but-unproven folk explanation, and "cost pressure + wire-service standardization + editors need bottom-cuttable copy" as the better-evidenced mechanism.

A genuinely useful nuance for framing PR-description/commit-message advice: AP's own internal guidance never asked for a rigid Five-Ws checklist. It asked for "clear, quick, complete, interesting" — the Five Ws are pedagogical scaffolding for journalism students, not the actual professional bar. This matters for the analogy to engineering writing: the fix for gate-status-first PR descriptions isn't "answer five fixed questions in order," it's "put the one fact a reader would want if they read only one sentence, first" — i.e., BLUF's framing is closer to what professional journalism actually optimizes for than the classroom mnemonic is.

BLUF's direct, explicitly-cited parallel to the inverted pyramid (Wikipedia's "BLUF... parallels the inverted pyramid structure used in journalism") is the strongest documented bridge from journalism craft to technical/business writing, and AR 25-50 is a rare case of a formal, codified, regulation-backed writing standard — not just an editorial convention — making the deductive-over-inductive structure literally a rule of engagement. Its pairing with mandatory active voice for accountability is also a useful transferable idea for commit messages/PRs: state the outcome, then say who/what changed it, unambiguously, before backfilling gate/process trivia.

"Burying the lede" is the ready-made, widely understood name for the failure mode this whole line of research is chasing: a PR description or commit message that opens with CI status, checklist trivia, or process narration and defers "what actually changed and why it matters" to paragraph three is burying the lede in the literal, named journalistic sense — not just "bad writing," but a century-and-a-half-old documented anti-pattern with an established vocabulary and an established fix.
