## CLAIMS

- In a 2004 internal email, Jeff Bezos banned PowerPoint (and any other slide-oriented presentation) at Amazon and required narratively structured, full-sentence memos instead. [inc-bezos-how-to-think]
- Bezos's stated reasoning, repeated in his 2017 Amazon shareholder letter: "The narrative structure of a good memo forces better thought and better understanding of what's more important than what, and how, things are related." [bezos-2017-shareholder-letter]
- Bezos explicitly named the mechanism of failure for the format he banned: "PowerPoint-style presentations somehow give permission to gloss over ideas, flatten out any sense of relative importance, and ignore the interconnectedness of ideas." [bezos-2017-shareholder-letter]
- Bezos generalized the ban beyond the specific tool PowerPoint to any bulleted format: "If someone builds a list of bullet points in Word, that would be just as bad as PowerPoint." [bezos-2017-shareholder-letter]
- The Amazon meeting format that replaced slide decks is a roughly six-page narrative memo, read silently by all attendees for about 30 minutes at the start of the meeting (Bezos calls this "study hall"), followed by discussion. [cnbc-six-page-memos]
- Bezos's stated rationale for silent-reading time rather than a live pitch: without it, "the executives, like high school kids, will try to bluff their way through the meeting" — i.e., a live presentation format lets a presenter's confidence substitute for the presenter having actually done the analysis. [cnbc-six-page-memos]
- Bezos described the memo-writing process itself as iterative and slow, not a single draft: "The great memos are written and re-written, shared with colleagues who are asked to improve the work, set aside for a couple of days, and then edited again with a fresh mind... They simply can't be done in a day or two." He estimated a great memo "probably should take a week or more." [cnbc-six-page-memos]
- Separately from Amazon, 3M's Gordon Shaw (executive director of planning and international, and past president of the Conference Board's Council of Strategic Planning Executives) led an internal shift in the 1990s away from bullet-point strategic business plans toward narrative "strategic stories," published as "Strategic Stories: How 3M Is Rewriting Business Planning," Harvard Business Review, May–June 1998, co-authored with Robert Brown and Philip Bromiley (both University of Minnesota). [hbr-strategic-stories-3m]
- Shaw's stated root cause, after reviewing hundreds of 3M business plans over several years: bullet-point plans "usually aren't anything more than lists of 'good things to do'" that "rarely... reflect deep thought or inspire commitment," because the list format "doesn't specify critical relationships between the points" and "can't demonstrate how the goals will be achieved." [hbr-strategic-stories-3m]
- 3M's replacement format required plans to be written as a narrative with a classic story structure — a stage that is set, a conflict introduced, and a resolution presented — reasoning that only a connected narrative, not a bullet list, can show cause-and-effect logic between a strategy's parts. [hbr-strategic-stories-3m]
- 3M separately justified the narrative approach on a distinct, non-logic-related mechanism: when readers/implementers "can locate themselves in the story," their sense of commitment and involvement increases — a motivational, not just clarity, argument for prose over bullets. [hbr-strategic-stories-3m]
- Both Amazon's and 3M's public rationales converge on the same specific mechanism, independently arrived at roughly six years apart (3M, HBR 1998; Amazon, internal 2004 / public 2017 letter): a bulleted list can state conclusions or action items without ever being forced to state the causal or logical connection between them, while full-sentence narrative prose cannot omit that connective logic without becoming visibly incoherent to the writer and reader. [bezos-2017-shareholder-letter] [hbr-strategic-stories-3m]

## SYNTHESIS

The useful finding here isn't "Amazon doesn't like PowerPoint" (widely known) — it's that Amazon and 3M independently named the *same specific failure mechanism* for bulleted formats, from different domains (product/eng strategy meetings vs. corporate strategic planning) and different decades of origin (3M's shift predates Bezos's ban by ~6 years in public record, per the 1998 HBR piece vs. the 2004 internal email). Both leaders arrived at: a bullet list can juxtapose a conclusion next to its "supporting" points without ever forcing the writer to state *how* one implies the other. The logical connective tissue — the word "because," "therefore," "which means," "but" — is exactly what a bullet format lets a writer omit, and exactly what full-sentence prose cannot omit without reading as a non sequitur to the writer themselves during drafting. Bezos's "permission to gloss over ideas... and ignore the interconnectedness of ideas" and Shaw's "doesn't specify critical relationships between the points" are the same claim in two vocabularies.

This is directly transferable to the standing complaint about bullet-fragment PR descriptions: a PR description that's a bullet list of "what changed" items can be complete-looking while omitting *why* each change was necessary or how the changes relate to each other — the exact defect Bezos and Shaw both diagnosed. The fix implied by both primary sources is not "write more bullets with more detail" but "force at least one full sentence that states the causal claim: X, because Y, therefore Z." Neither source claims bullets are always wrong (Bezos still uses structured docs with headers/sections; 3M's narrative plans still have discrete parts) — the claim is narrower and more defensible: *the causal/logical connective language between points must not be optional*, and prose structurally forces it while bullets structurally permit skipping it.

A secondary, distinct mechanism surfaced only in the Amazon material (not present in the 3M source): Bezos's "bluff their way through the meeting" line is about a *live-presentation* failure mode — a confident verbal pitch can paper over analysis that was never actually done, a problem that silent collective reading defeats by removing the presenter's ability to perform confidence in place of rigor. This is a different claim from the bullet-vs-prose one (it's about live speech vs. written artifact, not about list vs. sentence) — worth keeping separate if reused, since conflating them overstates what either source actually supports.

Note on scope versus the existing corpus entry `writing-craft/inverted-pyramet-and-bluf-...` (actual filename: `inverted-pyramid-and-bluf-solve-the-same-problem-a-reader-who-may-stop-at-any-point.md`): that entry covers AR 25-50 and BLUF's "conclusion-first" structural claim (deductive vs. inductive ordering) and journalism's inverted pyramid. This entry is a different, complementary claim — not about *ordering* (conclusion first) but about *format* (prose vs. bullets) and a specific causal mechanism (bullets let you skip stating logical connections). A complete PR-description standard would want both: BLUF ordering (state the outcome first) AND narrative connective tissue (state why, not just what) — they are independent axes, and conflating "put it first" with "write it in prose" would understate the case AR 25-50, Amazon, and 3M each make on their own terms.
