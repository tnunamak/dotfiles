## CLAIMS

- The term "curse of knowledge" originates in economics: Colin Camerer, George Loewenstein, and Martin Weber, "The Curse of Knowledge in Economic Settings: An Experimental Analysis," *Journal of Political Economy* 97(5), 1989, pp. 1232-1254. [camerer-loewenstein-weber-1989]
- The 1989 paper's finding is that better-informed subjects cannot fully ignore their private information even when doing so would benefit them (e.g., predicting a less-informed market's judgments); market forces (repeated trading, incentives) reduced but did not eliminate the effect, cutting it by roughly half. [camerer-loewenstein-weber-1989]
- The paper explicitly frames curse of knowledge as a violation of the standard economic assumption that better-informed agents can accurately reconstruct the judgments of less-informed agents. [camerer-loewenstein-weber-1989]
- The popular "tapper-listener" demonstration comes from Elizabeth Newton's 1990 Stanford psychology dissertation, commonly cited as *Newton, L. (1990). Overconfidence in the communication of intent: Heard and unheard melodies*, unpublished doctoral dissertation, Stanford University — sources show some inconsistency in the exact cited title. [newton-tapper-listener-1990]
- In Newton's experiment, "tappers" tapped out the rhythm of a well-known song (from a list of 25, e.g. "Happy Birthday to You," "The Star-Spangled Banner") on a table; "listeners" tried to name the song. [newton-tapper-listener-1990]
- Tappers predicted listeners would guess correctly about 50% of the time; listeners actually identified only 3 of 120 tapped songs, roughly 2.5%. [newton-tapper-listener-1990]
- The tapper-listener study was not itself published as a standalone peer-reviewed paper under that framing; it became widely known primarily through Chip and Dan Heath's 2007 book *Made to Stick: Why Some Ideas Survive and Others Die*, which uses it as the opening illustration of the curse of knowledge. [made-to-stick-secondary]
- Steven Pinker devotes Chapter 3 of *The Sense of Style* (2014) to "The Curse of Knowledge," calling it "the chief contributor to opaque writing" in at least one interview framing, and elsewhere in the book calling it "a pervasive drag on the strivings of humanity, on a par with corruption, disease, and entropy." [psych-science-pinker-observer] [supersummary-ch3-4]
- Pinker's diagnosis: the writer doesn't realize the reader hasn't mastered the field's jargon, can't infer missing steps that feel obvious to the writer, and can't visualize a scene that is vivid to the writer — so the writer skips explaining jargon, skips logical steps, and omits concrete detail. [supersummary-ch3-4] [psych-science-pinker-observer]
- Pinker explicitly warns that merely trying harder to "keep the reader in mind" is not reliable, because no one can directly access another person's actual state of knowledge; imagined perspective-taking alone does not fix the bias. [supersummary-ch3-4]
- Pinker's concrete countermeasures, in order of what he treats as most load-bearing: (1) show an early draft to a real representative reader and note where they get confused; (2) do a dedicated revision pass whose only goal is clarity, without adding new content; (3) cut jargon and abbreviations, replacing them with plain synonyms; (4) when a technical term must be used, define it briefly; (5) prefer concrete, sensory language over abstraction (his example: "a quiet room" over "conditions of good acoustic isolation"). [supersummary-ch3-4] [psych-science-pinker-observer]
- Pinker connects the curse of knowledge to under-explained logical connectives: he argues writers more often err by supplying too few connective words (because they take relationships between ideas as self-evident) than by over-explaining, and advises "when in doubt, connect." [supersummary-ch3-4]
- A direct, controlled empirical test of debiasing exists: Hinds, P. J. (1999), "The Curse of Expertise: The Effects of Expertise and Debiasing Methods on Predictions of Novice Performance," *Journal of Experimental Psychology: Applied* 5(2), 205-221. [hinds-1999-curse-of-expertise]
- Hinds (1999) found that experts were worse than intermediate-level users at predicting how long novices would take on a task, and that experts were resistant to debiasing techniques intended to reduce their underestimation of novice difficulty; intermediate-level users predicted novice performance more accurately than either novices or experts. [hinds-1999-curse-of-expertise]
- Camerer et al. (1989) separately established that the underlying bias is not eliminated by incentives or outcome feedback alone, in the original economic-forecasting paradigm. [camerer-loewenstein-weber-1989]
- Google's public "Technical Writing One" course (developers.google.com/tech-writing/one) teaches the curse of knowledge directly to software engineers, defining it as "their expert understanding of a topic ruins their explanations to newcomers." [google-tech-writing-audience]
- Google's course uses a worked exercise: it presents an example paragraph describing C as "a mid-level language with fine-grained programmer control" first to an audience of physicians with no programming background, then to an audience of Python-familiar CS undergraduates — showing the paragraph still suffers from unexplained jargon (pointers, memory manipulation) even for the more technical audience, because "closely related knowledge" is not the same as "knowledge of the specific subject." [google-tech-writing-audience]
- Google's course's concrete techniques: match vocabulary to the audience's actual (not assumed) expertise; explicitly define abbreviations and implementation details familiar to the writer's own team but not to outsiders; prefer simple/common words over complex or obsolete ones; and strip idioms/culturally-specific metaphors because international readers and machine translation handle plain language better. [google-tech-writing-audience]

## SYNTHESIS

Two separate, independently-replicated research lines converge on the same bias under the same name, which makes "curse of knowledge" unusually well-grounded as citable jargon (ironic, given the topic): Camerer/Loewenstein/Weber (1989, economics, JPE) proved it in incentivized market forecasting, and Newton (1990, psychology, Stanford dissertation, popularized via *Made to Stick*) proved it in a communication task. Pinker's contribution in *The Sense of Style* is not new data — it's a diagnosis that reframes an economics/psychology bias as *the* generative mechanism behind bad academic and technical prose, and a set of concrete, prioritized countermeasures. His single most important methodological point, easy to miss in summaries, is that *trying harder to imagine the reader* does not work — the fix has to be structural (a real reader, a dedicated clarity-only revision pass), not an act of will or empathy.

Hinds (1999) is the load-bearing empirical caveat for anyone designing a debiasing procedure: it directly tested "debiasing techniques" against expert overconfidence about novice difficulty and found experts resistant to them, while intermediate-level users (people one or two steps removed from novice, not full experts) were the best predictors of novice difficulty. This has a direct, practical implication for how an AI agent or technical writer should self-correct: asking the *expert* (or the model that just wrote the jargon-laden draft) to imagine a naive reader and try again is the least reliable fix in the literature. What has evidence behind it is (a) a genuinely separate, less-expert reader/reviewer — Pinker's "show a draft to a representative reader," Google's worked contrast-audience exercise — or (b) a structural, content-frozen clarity-only revision pass, which forces re-derivation of explanations rather than relying on imagined empathy.

For technical writing specifically (including AI-agent-authored docs/PRs/READMEs), Google's Technical Writing One course is the most directly citable software-industry source: it teaches the curse of knowledge explicitly to engineers (not just professional writers), and its worked exercise ("does this paragraph still assume too much even for a CS-adjacent audience?") is a reusable template for a self-check: pick a term, ask whether the *closest* plausible outside reader (not the most naive one) would still stumble on it. That "closest adjacent reader" framing lines up with Hinds's finding that intermediate-expertise judges outperform experts at estimating novice difficulty — so if a fully naive test reader isn't available, a moderately-informed one is a better proxy than the author's own imagination.

Practical implication for writing-craft rules/skills: any "explain like I wasn't in the room" or "assume nothing" checklist item should be understood as a second-best substitute for an actual outside reader, not a proven-sufficient debiasing technique on its own — the literature's strongest finding is that awareness and imagined perspective-taking are only weakly effective, and real contact with a less-informed party (or a real second-pass reviewer with fresh eyes) is what has evidence behind it.
