# The EU Data Act and PDPP

Date: 2026-08-07
Status: research note, not part of the normative spec

## Purpose

PDPP's wire protocol makes zero normative assumptions that the data subject is a natural person. Section 2 of spec-core.md defines the User as "the person whose data is being accessed," but nothing in the grant, selection request, manifest, or resource-server interface schemas requires that the authenticated account map to a human. This note asks how the EU Data Act, Regulation (EU) 2023/2854, relates to PDPP given that starting point.

Primary source: Regulation (EU) 2023/2854 of 13 December 2023, fetched from EUR-Lex (CELEX 32023R2854), PDF route, converted with `pdftotext`. All article and recital numbers below are verified against that text.

## 1. What the Data Act actually requires

The Data Act applies from **12 September 2025** (Article 50, second paragraph: "It shall apply from 12 September 2025"). The Article 3(1) design obligation on connected products applies only to products placed on the market after 12 September 2026 (Article 50, third paragraph).

**Article 3** requires connected products and related services to be designed so that product data and related-service data are, "by default, easily, securely, free of charge, in a comprehensive, structured, commonly used and machine-readable format, and, where relevant and technically feasible, directly accessible to the user" (Article 3(1)). This is a design-time, access-by-default obligation aimed at the manufacturer, not a runtime API contract between named parties.

**Article 4** governs the fallback path when direct access is not available. Where data cannot be directly accessed from the product or service, "data holders shall make readily available data... accessible to the user without undue delay, of the same quality as is available to the data holder, easily, securely, free of charge... and, where relevant and technically feasible, continuously and in real-time. This shall be done on the basis of a simple request through electronic means where technically feasible" (Article 4(1)). Article 4(5) limits what a data holder may demand to verify user status: "a data holder shall not require that person to provide any information beyond what is necessary," and log retention on access requests is capped at what is "necessary for the sound execution of the user's access request and for the security and maintenance of the data infrastructure."

**Article 5** extends the same obligation to a third party the user names: "Upon request by a user, or by a party acting on behalf of a user, the data holder shall make available readily available data... to a third party without undue delay, of the same quality as is available to the data holder, easily, securely, free of charge to the user, in a comprehensive, structured, commonly used and machine-readable format and, where relevant and technically feasible, continuously and in real-time" (Article 5(1)). Article 5(3) carves gatekeepers designated under the Digital Markets Act (Regulation (EU) 2022/1925, Article 3) out of eligibility as a receiving third party.

**Articles 8-9** set the business-to-business terms for that third-party flow. Article 8(1) requires the data holder to agree arrangements with the data recipient "under fair, reasonable and non-discriminatory terms and conditions and in a transparent manner." Article 9(1) requires any compensation to be "non-discriminatory and reasonable and may include a margin," with Article 9(4) capping compensation at cost recovery when the recipient is an SME or not-for-profit research organization.

**Articles 33 and 35** address interoperability, and neither names a protocol. Article 33(1) sets four essential requirements for data-space participants: machine-readable description of dataset content and licensing terms (33(1)(a)); public, consistent description of data structures, formats, and vocabularies (33(1)(b)); sufficiently described technical access means "such as application programming interfaces... to enable automatic access and transmission of data between parties, including continuously, in bulk download or in real-time" (33(1)(c)); and, "where applicable, the means to enable the interoperability of tools for automating the execution of data sharing agreements, such as smart contracts" (33(1)(d)). Article 33(3)-(4) route conformity through harmonised standards the Commission requests from European standardisation organizations, not through a designated existing spec. Article 35 sets parallel interoperability requirements specifically for switching between data processing services (cloud/edge), again deferred to harmonised standards and common specifications published in a "central Union standards repository" (Article 35(8)). Recital 90 is the one place a standard is named at all, and it is a conceptual reference model rather than a candidate protocol: "The ISO/IEC 19941:2017 is an important international standard constituting a reference for the achievement of the objectives of this Regulation, as it contains technical considerations clarifying the complexity of such a process."

**Article 36** sets essential requirements for smart contracts used to execute data-sharing agreements: robustness and access control, "internal functions which can reset or instruct the contract to stop or interrupt the operation" (Article 36(1)(b)), data archiving and continuity on termination, access control at the governance and contract layers, and consistency with the underlying agreement's terms.

## 2. Who it binds, and for whom

**Article 2(12)** defines "user" as "a natural or legal person that owns a connected product or to whom temporary rights to use that connected product have been contractually transferred, or that receives related services." The user is not restricted to natural persons. A business that owns a connected product or subscribes to a related service is a Data Act "user" in exactly the same sense as a consumer who owns one.

**Article 2(13)** defines "data holder" as "a natural or legal person that has the right or obligation... to use and make available data." **Article 2(14)** defines "data recipient" as "a natural or legal person, acting for purposes which are related to that person's trade, business, craft or profession, other than the user of a connected product or related service, to whom the data holder makes data available, including a third party following a request by the user to the data holder." Both roles admit legal persons without qualification; "data recipient" is explicitly scoped to acting for trade/business/craft/profession purposes.

Separately, **Article 2(23)** defines "consumer" as "any natural person who is acting for purposes which are outside that person's trade, business, craft or profession," and **Article 2(24)** defines "enterprise" as "a natural or legal person that... is acting for purposes which are related to that person's trade, business, craft or profession." The Regulation uses "consumer" and "enterprise" as narrower terms for specific provisions (Chapter II is titled "Business to Consumer and Business to Business Data Sharing"); "user" is the broader term that spans both.

The result is a triangle with three named roles: the user (Article 2(12)), the data holder (Article 2(13)), and the data recipient the user names (Article 2(14) and Article 5(1): "Upon request by a user, or by a party acting on behalf of a user, the data holder shall make available... data... to a third party"). This is structurally the same triangle PDPP's spec-core.md Section 2 describes as the User, the Data Source (holding the data), and the Client (the party the user authorizes to receive it), mediated by a grant.

Article 4(12) and Article 5(7) both flag one place the triangle gets more complicated than PDPP's grant model needs to: "Where the user is not the data subject whose personal data is requested," the data holder needs a GDPR Article 6 legal basis before releasing that data to the user or to the third party. The Data Act does not resolve this itself; it defers to GDPR. This matters because the Data Act's "user" (Article 2(12)) and GDPR's "data subject" (Article 2(11), cross-referencing GDPR Article 4(1)) are different roles that can diverge, for instance a company car's fleet manager (Data Act user) requesting data that includes an individual driver's (data subject) location history.

## 3. Where PDPP's mechanics fit, and where they do not

**Fit.** PDPP's grant, in Section 6 of spec-core.md, is the user's expressed, parameterized consent for a client to access specific data. That maps onto the Article 5(1) mechanic directly: "Upon request by a user... the data holder shall make available readily available data... to a third party." PDPP's `access_mode` field (`single_use` or `continuous`, Section 5) maps onto the Article 4(1)/Article 5(1) phrase "where relevant and technically feasible, continuously and in real-time" the same way spec-core.md's existing DMA table entry maps `continuous` access mode to DMA Article 6(9). PDPP's per-request enforcement, where the resource server filters records against a grant's stream, field, and time-range constraints (Section 8), is one way to implement the Article 4(1)/5(1) requirement that data be made available "of the same quality as is available to the data holder... in a comprehensive, structured, commonly used and machine-readable format." PDPP's record model (Section 4), flat relational streams with declared schemas and primary keys, is a candidate carrier for what Article 2(17) calls "readily available data... without disproportionate effort going beyond a simple operation": a resource server holding pre-collected or natively-served records under that model is already structured to answer a Article 4/5 request without a bespoke export job per request.

Article 4(5) and Article 5(4), which bar the data holder from demanding more than necessary to verify a person "qualifies as a user" and cap retained access-log data to what is operationally necessary, are compatible with PDPP's authorization model. PDPP's grant is scoped by an already-authenticated account; it does not require or preclude any particular identity-verification step beyond that authentication, so it does not conflict with the Article 4(5)/5(4) minimization rule.

**Does not fit.** Three groups of Data Act provisions are outside a disclosure protocol's scope by construction, not by omission. Article 8-9's FRAND/compensation terms for business-to-business data sharing (fair, reasonable, non-discriminatory pricing and terms) govern a commercial relationship between a data holder and a data recipient; PDPP defines the authorization and disclosure mechanics, not the business terms two parties strike around a grant. Article 4(6)-(8) and 5(9)-(11)'s trade-secret carve-outs, which let a data holder withhold or condition disclosure on confidentiality measures, are a substantive access-limitation policy a resource server operator would apply before or around a grant; PDPP's manifest (Section 7) can declare which streams or fields a connector exposes at all, but the trade-secret balancing test itself is a data-holder policy decision the protocol does not adjudicate. Article 36's smart-contract essential requirements (robustness, kill switches, archiving, access control, consistency with the underlying agreement) target automated on-chain or off-chain execution of the data-sharing agreement itself; PDPP has no smart-contract component and does not need one, since the grant is the agreement and the resource server is the enforcement point.

## 4. Honest caveats

No regulator has referenced PDPP in connection with the Data Act or any other instrument. This is unverified and stated as such because it cannot be verified as true; it is simply the absence of any evidence found.

The Data Act names no protocol for the Article 4/5 disclosure mechanism itself. Its interoperability provisions (Article 33, Article 35) are performance-oriented essential requirements that route to future harmonised standards and Commission-adopted common specifications, not to an existing named standard. The one specific standard named in the text, ISO/IEC 19941:2017 (recital 90), is a conceptual cloud-interoperability reference model cited for context on why interoperability is a cost-benefit problem, not a wire-format or protocol candidate a data holder could implement today to satisfy Article 4 or 5.

What follows in this note is adjacency, not endorsement: the observation that PDPP's grant/query mechanics are one workable way to satisfy an obligation the Data Act states abstractly, not a claim that the Data Act points to PDPP or that PDPP was built for the Data Act. The claim is stronger, not weaker, for the fact that PDPP's subject is an authenticated account rather than necessarily a natural person: Article 2(12)'s "user" is explicitly "a natural or legal person," so a protocol that assumes nothing about the subject's legal personhood fits the Article 2(12) definition without needing to special-case business accounts, in contrast to a hypothetical portability protocol built GDPR-first around a natural-person data subject.

## 5. So what

The Data Act is a tailwind PDPP can serve without renaming or re-missioning anything. PDPP already frames itself, per spec-core.md's existing relationship table, as informative alignment with GDPR Article 20 (a one-shot portability right for natural persons) and DMA Article 6(9) (continuous, real-time access for gatekeeper end users). The Data Act adds a third, complementary data point: a disclosure obligation whose "user" role is legal-person-inclusive by definition (Article 2(12)) and whose third-party-request flow (Article 5(1)) is structurally the owner/client/source triangle PDPP already implements. None of this requires PDPP to add Data Act-specific fields, claim compliance with a regulation whose interoperability provisions do not yet name an implementable spec, or narrow its subject model to fit a regulatory frame; it means PDPP's existing subject-neutral design already covers ground the Data Act's user definition opens up.

Can a business use PDPP? Yes: Article 2(12) defines "user" as "a natural or legal person," and PDPP's spec never narrows its authenticated-account subject to a natural person, so a business account authorizing a client to access its connected-product or related-service data through PDPP's grant and query mechanics is exercising the same kind of request Article 5(1) describes, without the protocol needing any change to accommodate it.
